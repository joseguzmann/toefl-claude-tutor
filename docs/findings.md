# Findings: what we learned about how one learner fails

These patterns came out of eight weeks of measuring one student (native Spanish speaker, strong
technical English). **They are hypotheses to test with your own data, not rules.** The tutor uses
them as a starting point and should drop any that your measurements don't support.

---

## Writing

**The preposition in the prompt is already on the screen.** Twice the student wrote the wrong
preposition (`in social media`) with the right one (`on social media`) printed four times in the
classmates' posts. The topic of an Academic Discussion almost always appears in the posts with its
preposition attached: **copy it from there.**

**Compound nouns remove the decision.** `members of the club` → `club members`. When the
preposition is the risk, stacking nouns avoids the choice instead of trying to get it right.

**More words isn't better.** ETS sample responses were around 113–130 words, with roughly the same
error rate as the student's. A discussion response of 194 words stated the thesis four times at
27.7 words per sentence and stayed at 4/5. The rubric asks you to *elaborate*; restating isn't
elaborating. What lifted it was **one concrete example developed over three sentences.**

**Answer what the prompt says.** Twice a score was lost to content, not language: inventing a
profession for a classmate in the discussion, and choosing a plan with a reason that contradicted
the email scenario.

**"It depends" belongs to Speaking, not Writing.** The discussion rubric rewards a clear position.

## Listening

**The option that repeats a word from the audio is usually the trap. The correct one is usually
the paraphrase.** 5 of 5 errors at one point, then 8 of 8 correct once applied as a rule. With
partial comprehension, the option built from the one sentence you fully understood is the most
dangerous one.

**Function questions fail more than detail questions.** "Why does the speaker mention X?" and
"What is the main point?" accounted for most misses. Academic talks go general → specific, and
signposts like *"Researchers have found that…"*, *"Interestingly…"*, *"The key is…"* mark where the
question will come from.

**It's which vocabulary, not speed or length.** In one lecture split into 13 sentences, the student
fully understood the four most Latin-based ones (one was 31 words long) and only half-understood a
6-word sentence without a single cognate (`haunted`, `impending`, `doom`). For a Romance-language
speaker with technical input, **the Germanic everyday layer of English** is the gap:
`luggage`, `goggles`, `fines`, `chip and PIN`, `receipt`.

## Speaking · Listen and Repeat

**The scoring tolerates small losses, not stopping.** The official rubric (Test Overview PDF)
allows one or two function words missing or changed at the top score, and the drop from 3 to 2 is
defined by not producing a full sentence. **Never stop. Close the sentence even if a word is missing.**
In long sentences, losing one content word and reaching the end still scores well.

**The word that drops is the adjective.** Nine of nine measured losses were adjectives (`shared`,
`important`, `fresh`, `different`…). Without it the sentence is still grammatical, so short-term
memory lets it go first. Choose shadowing sentences **dense in adjectives**.

**Words move, they don't vanish.** A word heard once can detach from its slot and land somewhere
else: in one set `hole` took three slots that weren't its own. Exam sets are always on a single
topic, so this repetition has to be trained, not avoided.

**Small words are heard, not just spoken.** `a`, `the`, `your`, `their` are unstressed in English
and swapped for each other (`your station` → `the station`), and ETS counts it. In Spanish the
article is never reduced, so there's no habit of listening for it.

**Some errors are pronunciation, not listening.** `levels` came out as `labels`: in Spanish `b` and
`v` are the same sound. The fix is physical (upper teeth on lower lip for `v`), not lexical.

**The first set of the day is the one that measures.** Third sets dropped ~8 points out of 35.

## Speaking · Take an Interview

**Silence costs more than grammar.** Five seconds of silence before starting, in three out of four
answers, was 11% of the available time. The answer that started immediately was the best one.

**Default preposition when speaking: `on`.** 5 of 6 spoken preposition errors were `on`. You don't
need to remember the right one for every word; it's enough to distrust `on`.

**A form fixed in writing doesn't transfer to speech by itself.** At 11:30 the student corrected
`follow a degree` → `do a degree` in writing; at 11:50, speaking, said `following a course`. Same
word, same day, twenty minutes later. That's the argument for a daily Interview.

**A coherent answer can hide a listening failure.** One answer was fluent and on topic but missed
what the question asked (`take part in`). Only the student's own report revealed it. Ask them.

## Reading

**Counting letters doesn't eliminate; the phrase does.** In Complete the Words, 3 of 5 wrong
answers had exactly the right number of letters.

**Reading and Listening are adaptive.** The first module (35 of 50 Reading questions, 32 of 47
Listening) is the same for everyone and decides the difficulty of the second. There is no warm-up:
the first questions weigh more than their number suggests.

## The student

**Their self-assessment was harsher than the data.** *"I don't understand anything the audio says"*,
about a lecture where they fully understood 4 sentences, half-understood 9, and missed none
entirely. Listening ended at 6.0.

**Their corrections of the tutor were almost always right.** Every blunt complaint produced a change
of method that worked.
